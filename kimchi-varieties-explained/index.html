Kimchi Varieties Explained

An overview of common kimchi types beyond cabbage kimchi, plus the Kimjang tradition behind Korea's seasonal kimchi-making culture.

K-Culture ExplainedOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Most people outside Korea picture one thing when they hear "kimchi": the red, spicy, fermented cabbage served as a side dish. That version, baechu kimchi, is real and common, but it's only one of many varieties that exist across Korean cuisine. Korea.net's own reference material on kimchi lists several distinct types, each built around a different base vegetable.

Beyond cabbage: the main varieties

According to Korea.net's official overview, common kimchi varieties include:

  • Cabbage kimchi (baechu kimchi), the most widely recognized version internationally.
  • Diced radish kimchi (kkakdugi), made from cubed Korean radish rather than cabbage leaves.
  • Cucumber kimchi (oi-sobagi), typically made by stuffing cucumbers with seasoned filling.
  • Leaf mustard kimchi (gat kimchi), using mustard greens instead of cabbage or radish.
  • Green onion kimchi (pa kimchi), built around whole or bundled green onions.
  • Radish water kimchi, a lighter, more liquid-based variety compared to the drier, seasoned types above.

Each variety uses a different vegetable as its base, but most share a similar seasoning foundation involving red pepper powder, fermented seafood sauce known as jeotgal, garlic, ginger, and salt, adjusted depending on the vegetable and regional preference.

Kimjang: the seasonal tradition behind the food

Kimchi isn't just a dish; the practice of making it in large batches has its own name and cultural weight. Kimjang refers to the Korean tradition of preparing large quantities of kimchi, typically in late autumn, to last a household through the winter months. According to Korea.net's reference on Kimjang culture, the main ingredients used during this seasonal preparation are napa cabbage and radish, seasoned with red pepper powder, jeotgal, salt, garlic, and ginger, the same core seasoning base shared across most kimchi varieties.

Kimjang was formally recognized by UNESCO in December 2013, when "Kimjang, Making and Sharing Kimchi in the Republic of Korea" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The nomination document, filed by the Republic of Korea, described Kimjang as following a yearly cycle: households procure shrimp, anchovy, and other seafood for salting and fermenting in spring, buy sea salt for brine in summer, dry and grind red chili peppers into powder in late summer, and then carry out the actual large-batch kimchi-making in late autumn, timed so that every household has enough kimchi to last through the winter.

Why Kimjang is about more than preserving food

UNESCO's inscription decision specifically noted that Kimjang is valued not only as food preparation but as a practice that reinforces Korean identity and strengthens family and community cooperation. The practice of households exchanging kimchi with neighbors during Kimjang season is part of what the inscription highlighted as reinforcing a sense of solidarity and shared identity, beyond the practical function of stocking food for winter. A 2012 survey by Korea's Cultural Heritage Administration, cited in Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs materials on the UNESCO inscription, found that roughly 90 percent of Koreans either make kimchi themselves or take part in Kimjang in some form, which is part of why the tradition was recognized as something transmitted broadly through everyday family life rather than practiced only by a small group.

Regional and family variation

Specific methods and exact ingredient ratios for Kimjang vary by region and even by individual family, and these variations are often treated as a kind of family heritage, commonly passed down from a mother-in-law to a daughter-in-law according to the UNESCO nomination file. This means that even within one country, there's no single "correct" recipe. The seasoning base stays broadly consistent, but proportions of chili powder, choice of seafood sauce, and the exact vegetables used can shift meaningfully from one region or household to another.

Why this matters if you're trying kimchi for the first time

If your only exposure to kimchi has been the pre-packaged cabbage version sold outside Korea, trying a radish-based kkakdugi or a lighter radish water kimchi can feel like an entirely different food, since the texture and spice level differ substantially from baechu kimchi. Recognizing that kimchi is a category of fermented, seasoned vegetable dishes, not one single recipe, makes it easier to understand why Korean households often serve several different kimchi varieties at the same meal rather than just one.

Key takeaways

  • Kimchi includes multiple distinct varieties, including cabbage, radish, cucumber, mustard leaf, and green onion versions, not just one standard recipe.
  • Most varieties share a seasoning base of red pepper powder, fermented seafood sauce, garlic, ginger, and salt.
  • Kimjang, the seasonal practice of making large batches of kimchi for winter, was recognized by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2013.
  • Roughly 90 percent of Koreans reported making kimchi or participating in Kimjang, according to a Cultural Heritage Administration survey cited by Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.
  • Exact Kimjang recipes vary by region and family, often passed down across generations rather than fixed nationally.
